# Supplemental: Community or Identity

## The essay
- Prompts about community, identity, background, perspective, or the contribution the student would bring. Lengths commonly run 200 to 350 words. The reader wants to understand the student's lived experience and how it shapes what they would add to a campus.
- Use the prompt the student supplies. Some prompts ask about a community the student belongs to, others about a difference they made, others about a perspective or value.

## What strong essays do
- Ground the topic in specific, lived detail: a person, place, practice, or moment, not a category label.
- Connect identity or community to values and to action: what the student does, notices, or contributes because of it.
- Show a distinct individual within the community, not a spokesperson for it.
- Look forward: what the student would bring to and gain from a campus community.

## Common weaknesses
- Describing the community in general terms with no scene.
- Relying on a label or stereotype instead of the student's own experience.
- Trauma used for effect, or hardship exaggerated, or hardship told without agency or reflection.
- Claiming contribution without a concrete example.
- Listing identities with no depth on any one.

## How to calibrate the analysis
- Authenticity and specific detail matter most. Do not reward or penalize a topic for how serious it sounds.
- Do not assume a hardship narrative is stronger. Quiet experiences can be excellent.
- Be respectful and neutral about identity. Assess writing and reflection, never the identity itself, and never ask the student to disclose more than they choose to.
- If the prompt asks about contribution, check that action and impact are visible.

## Suggestions worth offering
- Ask for a single moment that shows the community or identity in action.
- Suggest moving from "I am part of" to "here is what I do, notice, or carry."
- Suggest one specific, forward-looking sentence about contribution.
- Teach technique with unrelated scenarios. Never write the essay or invent experiences.
- Exercise to try: ask for one contribution or encounter and how it shaped the student's perspective.
- Never require demographic disclosure, never treat any identity as more valuable than another, and never presume background or eligibility from the essay.
