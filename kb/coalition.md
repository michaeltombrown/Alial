# Coalition / Scoir Essay

## The essay
- The Coalition application (delivered through Scoir) asks for a personal essay with its own set of prompts, with a recommended length in the 500 to 650 word range. The official page offers six broad options: a personal experience, interests, impact on others, a belief that was challenged, obstacles and success, or a topic of the student's choice. Individual school requirements may differ. Use the prompt the student supplies.
- Treat it like a personal statement: the reader wants to understand who the student is beyond grades and activities.

## What strong essays do
- Center on one story or idea told with specific detail and honest reflection.
- Reveal character through behavior, choices, and what the student cares about, not through claims.
- Keep a distinct voice. Humor, curiosity, and quiet observation are as valid as high stakes.
- Connect the experience to who the student is becoming.

## Common weaknesses
- Trying to cover several experiences and explaining none of them.
- Generic life lessons, borrowed quotations, or a thesis statement in place of a story.
- Responding to the surface of the prompt without saying why the moment matters to this student.
- Describing a team, family, or community without showing the student's own role.

## How to calibrate the analysis
- Apply the same standards as a Common App personal statement, adjusted for the supplied prompt.
- If the prompt asks about contribution or a problem to solve, check that the student's own actions and reasoning are visible, not just the outcome.
- Do not require hardship or a transformation. Meaningful ordinary experiences can be excellent.
- Check length against the recommended range and note large gaps.

## Suggestions worth offering
- Ask the student which detail only they would have noticed.
- Suggest replacing summary sentences with one concrete scene.
- Point out where reflection could move from "what happened" to "why it mattered to me."
- Never supply replacement text. Teach technique with unrelated examples.
- Exercise to try: map the draft to one prompt, highlight the details only this applicant could supply, then cut the generic claims.
- Never presume a student's income, eligibility, or background from the essay.
