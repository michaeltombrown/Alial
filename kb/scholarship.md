# Scholarship Essay

## The essay
- Written for a scholarship or honors program, usually 300 to 800 words, answering the sponsor's own prompt. Readers are selection committees comparing many applicants against the scholarship's stated purpose and values.
- Use the prompt and any criteria the student supplies. If none are given, say you cannot judge fit to the award.

## What strong essays do
- Answer the prompt directly and tie the student's experience, goals, and values to what the award is for.
- Give concrete evidence of impact, initiative, or commitment: what the student did and what resulted.
- State clear goals and explain the reasoning behind them, including how the award would help when the prompt invites it.
- Stay honest, specific, and in the student's own voice.

## Common weaknesses
- A recycled personal statement that ignores the award's purpose.
- Claims of deserving or need without specifics, or exaggerated hardship.
- Goals described in general terms ("make a difference").
- A list of achievements with no reflection.
- Unproofread writing and missed instructions, which committees notice.

## How to calibrate the analysis
- Weight prompt fit and evidence of impact more than literary flourish, though clarity and voice still matter.
- Need or hardship is relevant only if the prompt invites it. When it does, check for agency, reflection, and dignity, not drama.
- Do not penalize an essay for lacking a narrative hook if it answers directly and persuasively.
- Check word limit and any stated requirements.

## Suggestions worth offering
- Ask the student to connect one sentence explicitly to the award's mission.
- Suggest replacing broad goals with the first concrete step already taken.
- Suggest tailoring rather than reusing an essay written for another purpose.
- Illustrate with unrelated scenarios. Never write the essay or invent details.
